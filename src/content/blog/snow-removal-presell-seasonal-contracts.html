The first storm is a bad time to discover that your route is half empty and every property manager in town is asking for immediate service. By then, your crew, equipment, and drive time are already under pressure. Seasonal contracts are won earlier, often in September, when clear offers and steady follow-up give customers time to choose you before snow is in the forecast.

Mistake 1: Waiting for Snow Before Calling Past Customers

Many snow removal owners leave last season's customer list untouched until the temperature drops. They assume customers will not care about plowing until a storm appears in the forecast, so the calls and texts wait. That habit pushes sales activity into the most chaotic part of the season.

Once snow is coming, every conversation feels urgent. Prospects want fast answers, existing customers need updates, equipment needs attention, and route decisions become harder to make. You may accept properties that do not fit your route simply because the phone is ringing. You may also lose good accounts because another contractor contacted them while you were still waiting.

September gives you a better setting for a useful sales conversation. A property owner can review service boundaries, trigger depths, access requirements, communication expectations, and renewal terms without a storm clock running. You can decide whether the property belongs on your route before making a commitment. That calmer process helps both sides understand what the contract covers.

Fix: Call and Text the List in September

Set a preseason start date and treat it as the beginning of your sales season. Build a list of past customers, open estimates, referral leads, and properties that fit the areas you already serve. Contact them with a simple message: you are planning the upcoming route, seasonal agreement reviews are open, and now is the time to confirm service.

Your message should give the prospect one clear next step. Ask them to reply for a review, request an estimate, or book a short call. Do not bury the request under a long explanation about your company. The goal of the first contact is to start a conversation while there is still room on the route.

Work backward from the date when routes must be finalized. Reserve time each week for outreach, site visits, estimates, and follow-up. Keep preseason selling separate from equipment preparation so it does not disappear behind maintenance work. Both matter, but a ready plow does not solve an empty route.

Make route fit part of the campaign. Group prospects by neighborhood or service area, then prioritize properties near existing stops. This lets sales decisions support operations. A contract is more valuable when your crew can serve it reliably without creating unnecessary travel or awkward route gaps.

Mistake 2: Taking Preseason Calls Without a Repeatable Intake

The prospect calls while you are in the truck or working on equipment. You jot down an address, promise to send something later, and move on. When you return to the lead, you cannot remember whether the site has sidewalks, a loading area, access limits, or special communication needs.

That loose phone intake produces a loose proposal. An agreement that says only “seasonal snow removal” leaves basic questions unanswered. The customer may wonder when service begins, what areas are included, whether sidewalks are covered, how ice service is handled, and who they should contact during an event.

The prospect then has to call again for details, or compare your unclear scope with another proposal that is easier to understand. For the owner, missing details also create risk after the sale. Your crew may arrive with one expectation while the customer has another.

Fix: Use the Same Intake Questions on Every Call

Keep a short intake form where you answer calls. Record the property address, decision maker, requested service areas, parking and loading zones, sidewalks, gates, access restrictions, snow placement, preferred communication method, and approval process. If you cannot finish the conversation, book a specific time to continue it instead of trusting yourself to remember.

Use those answers to build a proposal both the customer and your crew can follow. Identify service triggers, included and excluded work, customer responsibilities, communication method, agreement period, and what happens after acceptance. Use plain language. If a term needs a long verbal explanation every time, the proposal likely needs revision.

Present one recommended next step near the proposal. Tell the prospect how to approve it and whom to contact with questions. A clear approval path also gives you a useful reason to follow up. You are helping them complete a specific decision, not merely asking whether they saw your email.

Before sending, compare the proposal with your call notes. If the scope leaves either the customer or the crew guessing, settle the missing detail before the agreement goes out.

Mistake 3: Treating the Estimate as the End of the Sale

Sending a proposal is not the same as closing a contract. Property owners are busy, inboxes fill up, partners need to review documents, and decisions get postponed when there is no immediate weather pressure. Silence does not always mean rejection. Often it means your estimate is sitting behind another task.

Some owners avoid follow-up because they do not want to sound pushy. Others send one message asking whether the prospect has questions, then stop. That leaves a promising lead without guidance and makes it easy for a more consistent contractor to win the account.

Follow-up is especially important in September because the customer still has time to think. Your job is to keep the decision visible, answer concerns, and give the prospect a clear path forward. Professional persistence is not pressure when each contact is relevant and respectful.

Fix: Use a Simple Follow-Up Sequence Until You Get a Decision

Plan follow-up when you send the estimate, not after you notice the prospect has gone quiet. Record the date sent, the next contact date, the person responsible, and the current status. A basic spreadsheet or customer system is enough if everyone uses it consistently.

Vary the purpose of each contact. The first message can confirm that the proposal arrived. The next can ask whether the scope matches the property’s needs. Another can remind the prospect that route planning is underway and invite a final review. Use email, phone, or text according to the customer’s preference and the relationship you have established.

Keep messages short and specific. Refer to the property, name the decision that is pending, and offer one next step. For example, ask whether they want to review walkway coverage or whether they are ready to approve the agreement. Avoid repeated “just checking in” messages that give the customer no useful reason to respond.

Create clear statuses such as proposal sent, review scheduled, revision requested, approved, declined, and no response. Move every lead to the right status after each contact. This prevents good opportunities from getting lost and shows you exactly where preseason sales are slowing down.

Decide in advance how you will close the loop. After your planned sequence, send a polite final note that explains you are completing the route and asks for a yes or no. If the prospect is not ready, record that result rather than leaving the estimate open forever. You can learn more about how to follow up after an estimate and build a repeatable process with a lead follow-up sequence.

The deeper benefit of follow-up is better planning. As replies come in, you can see which route areas are filling, which proposals need changes, and where you still need outreach. That information helps you sell deliberately instead of reacting after the first storm.

A Practical Preseason Sales Rhythm

Combine the three fixes into one weekly rhythm. Start with targeted outreach, move qualified properties into a clear proposal process, and assign every open estimate a next action. Review the pipeline on the same day each week. Look for leads with no owner, no status, or no scheduled follow-up, because those are the opportunities most likely to disappear.

Keep the process manageable for a small operation. You do not need a complicated sales department. You need a list you trust, agreements that answer common questions, and a follow-up habit that continues while you prepare crews and equipment. Consistency in September gives you more control over the route you will operate when winter arrives.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I start selling seasonal snow removal contracts?

Start in September or at the point when you begin planning the upcoming route. Early outreach gives returning customers time to renew and new prospects time to review the property scope before forecasts create urgency.

What should a seasonal snow removal proposal include?

Include the property and service areas, service triggers, included and excluded work, access details, snow placement expectations, customer responsibilities, communication method, agreement period, and approval instructions. Adapt the scope to the property and your actual operation.

How often should I follow up after sending an estimate?

Use a planned sequence rather than an improvised schedule. Each contact should have a purpose, such as confirming receipt, reviewing scope, answering a concern, or requesting a final decision. Continue until the prospect approves, declines, or you close the loop.

How can I avoid sounding pushy during follow-up?

Be brief, specific, and helpful. Mention the property, explain why you are contacting them, and offer one clear next step. Respect a direct no, but do not assume that silence means the customer is not interested.
