Garage Doors Are a "Call Once Every Few Years" Business — That's the Problem

A good garage door lasts fifteen to thirty years. Springs go seven to ten. Openers run a decade or more. So when you replace a homeowner's broken spring, do clean work, and leave them happy, the honest truth is they may not need you again for years.

And when that day finally comes, they will not remember your name or find your invoice. They will type "garage door repair near me" into Google — the same search that brought them to you — and this time it might deliver your competitor. You paid to win that customer once, did excellent work, and got exactly one ticket out of the relationship.

That is the default economics of this trade, and most companies just accept it. The ones that grow past it treat the repair not as the end of the relationship but as the beginning of one — with three simple, mostly-automated moves: a maintenance cadence that brings the customer back, a review request that turns the finished job into marketing, and a referral ask timed for the moment they like you most.

None of it requires new skills, and all of it runs itself once set up. Here is how.

The Tune-Up Reminder That Brings Them Back

Garage doors genuinely benefit from annual service — this is not a manufactured upsell. Rollers wear, cables fray, tracks drift, springs lose tension, and the opener's safety-reverse should be tested every year. A tuned-up door runs quieter and its expensive parts last longer. Most homeowners have never been offered one.

So offer one — automatically. When a repair closes, the customer enters a reminder cadence; six to twelve months later they get a short text or email:

"Hi Sarah, it's Summit Garage Door — we replaced your torsion spring last fall. Doors we service do best with a quick annual tune-up: we lube the moving parts, check roller and cable wear, balance the springs, and test the opener's safety reverse. Want us to swing by this month? Book here: [link]"

A few things make garage door work unusually suited to this:

  • The other spring. When one spring on a two-spring door breaks, its twin has the same age and cycles on it. If the customer only replaced one, a reminder six months later is a favor, not a sales pitch.
  • Seasonal timing. Cold snaps are when tired springs break and stiff doors strain openers, so a fall "ready for winter" reminder and a spring check land when the customer can feel the relevance.
  • Small consumables. Opener backup batteries age out, remotes die, weather seals crack. Each is a small ticket — and a natural reason to be back in the driveway, where techs spot the worn rollers or fraying cable that becomes the real job.

Price the tune-up modestly. Its job is not margin — it is making sure that when the opener dies or they decide on a new door, there is no Google search at all. That replacement is a four-figure ticket, and it goes to whoever is top of mind.

Turn the Finished Repair Into a Five-Star Review

A garage door customer right after the job is one of the most reviewable customers in home services. You just ended a genuinely stressful problem — the car was trapped, the door would not close overnight — and the relief is real. But relief has a short shelf life: ask that evening and you catch gratitude at its peak; ask next week and the repair is old news.

The fix is a review request that sends itself a few hours after the job closes:

"Thanks for choosing Summit Garage Door today! If Mike took good care of you, would you take 30 seconds to share it? It genuinely helps our small business: [direct Google review link]"

Three details matter: send it the same day; name the tech, because customers review people more readily than companies; and link straight to the review form, not your profile page — every extra tap loses people. Referrals: The Neighbor With the Same Builder-Grade Door

A quirk of this trade that most owners sense but never act on: garage door failures cluster by street. Subdivisions go up in a season, every house gets the same builder-grade door and spring hardware, and those springs hit end-of-life on roughly the same clock. Replace a spring on Maple Court and odds are three neighbors are within a year or two of the same failure.

"We did a spring job on a cul-de-sac in March. By June we'd done three more houses on the same street — same builder, same doors, same tired springs. All three came from the first customer mentioning us. We didn't even ask. Imagine if we had."

You do not need to knock on the neighbors' doors — you need the customer you just helped to mention you. People mention companies that ask at the happiest moment: the first week or two after the repair, ideally right after a good review. A simple line in your check-in message does the work:

"One more thing — houses in your neighborhood were mostly built around the same time, so your neighbors' doors are aging right along with yours. If anyone mentions a noisy or stubborn door, we'd love the introduction."

No discount codes, no complicated referral program. Just a plain ask, timed well, that makes the customer feel smart for knowing a good garage door company.

Set It Once, Runs Itself

Put the pieces together and you get a short sequence that starts automatically every time a job closes:

  • Same day: Thank-you text with the direct review link, naming the tech.
  • Two weeks out: Quick check-in — "door still running smooth and quiet?" — with the neighborly referral line at the bottom.
  • Six to nine months out: Tune-up reminder with a booking link — if they replaced one spring, mention the other one.
  • Every spring and fall after that: Seasonal maintenance nudge until they book or opt out.

To build it yourself, you need customer names, numbers, and job dates in one place (a spreadsheet is a fine start); a business texting or AI automation platform that can send messages on a delay; and an online booking link so a reminder becomes a scheduled job without phone tag. Write the four messages once, in your own voice, and test the sequence on yourself before turning it on.

Four messages. An afternoon of setup. From then on, every repair quietly plants a review, a referral ask, and a future appointment while you are busy on the next job. Frequently Asked Questions

Is a tune-up really worth my time for a small ticket?

Not as a standalone job — and that is the wrong way to measure it. The tune-up is how you stay the customer's garage door company between big tickets: it keeps your name in their phone, puts a tech in the driveway where real wear gets spotted honestly, and means that when the opener dies or they replace the door, there is no competitive search — they just text you. Judge it by the replacement doors and opener installs it eventually feeds, not by the service fee.

Won't maintenance reminders annoy customers or feel like spam?

Not if they are honest, infrequent, and specific. One or two reminders a year, referencing the actual work you did ("we replaced your left spring last October") and a real maintenance need, reads as a professional running a real operation — like a dentist's cleaning reminder. Generic blasts and false urgency are what annoy people: skip the "LIMITED TIME" language, make opting out easy, and most customers will appreciate being remembered.

How soon after the job should the review request go out?

Same day — ideally within a few hours of the tech leaving. Gratitude after a solved emergency decays fast; a request that evening catches the customer while "they got my car out of the garage" is still the day's story. If the same-day text gets no response, one gentle nudge two or three days later is fine. After that, let it go — the two-week check-in still gets its chance.

My customer records are a mess. Where do I start?

Start with the next job, not the backlog. Turn the sequence on for every new customer from today forward and you will have a growing base on autopilot within a month. Then, in a slow week, mine old invoices for past spring and opener customers and send a one-time, honest re-introduction with a tune-up offer. Even a partial list beats letting every one of those relationships expire in silence.
